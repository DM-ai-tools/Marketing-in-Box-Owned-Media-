# PART 3 — FULL BLOG POST

## LinkedIn Company Page vs Personal Profile: Why Professional Services Firms Get This Backwards

**Meta description:** LinkedIn company page vs personal profile: why the algorithm favours people over pages, and what that means for professional services firms specifically.

---

A partner at your firm posts something rough from their phone — no design, no plan, maybe typed in an airport lounge between flights — and within two days it's done more than your last six months of company content combined. Meanwhile, the LinkedIn company page keeps ticking along: on-brand, approved, scheduled, and quietly ignored.

If that's happened at your firm, you've already lived the answer to the question this article is about to explain. The comparison isn't really "LinkedIn company page vs personal profile" as two equally valid style choices. It's a specific, measurable distribution mechanism — and once you understand how it actually works, the results you've been seeing stop looking like luck and start looking exactly like what you'd expect.

### The Pattern Every Firm Recognises, But Nobody Explains

Almost every marketing lead at a partner-led firm has a version of this story. It's rarely the flagship content — the whitepaper, the case study, the carefully shot video — that gets real engagement. It's the unscripted moment: a partner's reflection after a hard client conversation, a principal's take on a regulatory change typed out in ten minutes, a throwaway comment that somehow turns into three genuine replies from people who aren't already clients or colleagues.

The usual reaction is to treat this as a fluke, or worse, as evidence that "LinkedIn is unpredictable." It's neither. It's a distribution pattern repeating itself with enough consistency that it should be treated as data, not anecdote. And the reason it keeps happening is structural, not random — which means it's also fixable, not something to just wait out.

### How LinkedIn Actually Treats a Company Page vs a Personal Profile

Here's the mechanism, stated plainly: LinkedIn's distribution logic is built around a professional network of people, and it treats company pages as a fundamentally different, lower-priority content type within that network. A personal profile's post can appear in the feeds of first, second, and even third-degree connections, gets weighted more heavily by early engagement from real accounts, and benefits from LinkedIn's own incentive to keep the platform feeling like a network of people rather than a directory of brands. A company page post, by contrast, is shown almost exclusively to people who already follow that page — a smaller, more static audience with none of the compounding reach a personal post can pick up.

This isn't a secret or a leaked algorithm detail — it's consistent with how LinkedIn has publicly described its own platform for years: a place for professional relationships between people, with company pages functioning as more of a reference destination than a distribution engine. The practical result is exactly what you've probably observed: a company page can do everything "right" — consistent posting, decent design, sensible topics — and still underperform a rough personal post, because the two are competing on structurally different playing fields, not the same one.

### Why This Matters More in Professional Services Than Almost Any Other Category

This distribution mechanic affects every industry on LinkedIn, but it matters disproportionately for professional services — accounting, legal, engineering, consulting, financial advisory, architecture — for a reason that has nothing to do with the algorithm and everything to do with how these buying decisions actually get made.

In most professional services categories, the client isn't really hiring "the firm" as an abstract entity. They're hiring a specific person's judgement — the partner who'll actually run the engagement, the principal who'll sign off on the advice, the practitioner who'll be in the room when something goes wrong and needs to be handled well. Trust in this category is personal before it's institutional. A firm can have the most polished company page in its market and still lose a pitch to a smaller competitor, simply because the prospect had already been quietly following that competitor's lead partner on LinkedIn for months and felt like they already knew them.

That's the real stakes behind LinkedIn marketing for professional services specifically: it's not a content-quality problem, and it's not solved by hiring a better designer for the company page. It's a question of whether your firm's actual credibility — which lives in specific people — is showing up where your buyers are actually looking, or whether it's locked inside a channel LinkedIn itself treats as secondary.

### Four Signs Your Firm Is Publishing Into the Wrong Channel

Some of these will be obvious the moment you read them. Others only show up if you go looking.

**Signs that show up in your data**

The first sign is a gap between impressions and response — respectable reach on the company page, but almost no comments, DMs, or "I saw your post" conversations that translate into anything real. The second is a lopsided comparison: when you look back over the last quarter, any post from an individual profile that got genuine engagement outperformed the equivalent company-page content by a wide margin, not a narrow one. If you've never actually compared the two side by side, that's itself worth noting — most firms haven't, because the company page and any individual activity usually live in separate, unconnected reporting.

**Signs that show up in conversations**

The third sign is what happens in real client and referral conversations: if someone says "I've been seeing a lot of you on LinkedIn lately," can you say with confidence whose face they'd associate with that — a specific partner, a specific principal — or would it just be your logo? The fourth is a workload tell: if the person who currently manages your LinkedIn presence spends most of their time on the company page rather than helping two or three senior people show up as themselves, the effort is going into the channel with the lower ceiling.

None of these four signs, on their own, means your strategy has failed. They mean the same thing every time: the firm's real credibility and its published content are sitting in two different places, and LinkedIn's own distribution mechanics are quietly reporting the gap back to you, one flat quarter at a time.

### It's Not "Company Page vs Personal Profile" — It's Company Page Supporting Personal Profiles

The useful reframe here isn't "abandon the company page and go all-in on individuals." It's recognising that the two aren't in competition — they play different roles, and most firms have them backwards. The company page's job is to look credible to someone who's already decided to check you out (a prospect who's just been referred, a candidate researching your firm before an interview, a partner Googling you mid-negotiation). It was never built to be the primary discovery or trust-building mechanism, and treating it as one is asking a reference page to do a relationship-building job.

The individual profile's job is the opposite: to be the actual place where trust gets built, over months of a prospect quietly reading, well before they ever fill in a form. This is where personal branding on LinkedIn genuinely matters for an Australian professional services firm specifically — not as a vanity exercise, but as the mechanism that gets your actual expertise in front of the people already deciding, quietly, whether they trust you.

In practice, this means building content around **capture and amplify** rather than production from scratch: a short, structured conversation with the partner who already knows the answer to a client's question, turned into content by someone else, reviewed against agreed compliance boundaries, and published under that partner's own name — with the company page then resharing and supporting it, not competing with it. This is the exact operating model behind our full LinkedIn Marketing & Ads service, and it's designed specifically around the thing that kills most attempts at this: partner time. Done properly, it costs a senior person well under an hour a month, not a new ongoing content-writing job on top of billable work.

Two supporting tactics extend this once the foundation exists. **LinkedIn Thought Leader Ads** let you put real budget behind a named individual's organic post, running it to a tightly defined audience by job title and industry — a materially different (and, in our experience, more effective) exercise than boosting company-page content to a broad audience. And employee advocacy — making it simple for the rest of your team to share and engage with content from their own profiles — turns a firm's existing network of relevant connections into distribution you already own but probably aren't using. Both of these are secondary levers, though — they only compound once individual voice actually exists to amplify.

It's asking them to stop letting something that's already working evaporate the moment the meeting ends — which, per everything above, is usually the single highest-leverage move available before any paid spend, any new hire, or any broader strategy conversation is needed at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should we delete our LinkedIn company page?**
No — and this isn't an argument for that. The company page still matters as a credibility reference for anyone who's already decided to check your firm out, and it's often required for running paid campaigns and hosting basic firm information. The point isn't to remove it; it's to stop expecting it to do a job — building trust with people who haven't decided yet — that LinkedIn's own distribution mechanics were never going to let it do well.

**How many partners need to post personally for this to actually work?**
Fewer than most firms assume. Two or three consistently active, named voices is a realistic and genuinely effective starting point for most firms in the 20–150 person range — it doesn't require every partner participating, and trying to get universal buy-in on day one is usually what causes these initiatives to stall before they start.

**What if our partners don't want to post personally?**
This is a genuinely common blocker, and it's usually a time objection dressed up as a reluctance objection. The fix isn't persuasion — it's reducing the actual ask to something that survives a busy season: a short, structured conversation about something they already know well, turned into content by someone else, with their approval on the final version. Once the time cost is visibly small, the reluctance usually drops with it.

**Does this apply outside professional services?**
The underlying distribution mechanic — personal profiles reaching further than company pages — applies to every industry on LinkedIn. It matters most acutely in categories where the buying decision is personal and judgement-based, which is why professional services, consulting, and other advisory-heavy fields see the starkest version of this pattern. In more transactional B2B categories, the gap between the two channels tends to be real but somewhat less dramatic.

**How long before we'd see a difference?**
Results vary by firm, by how consistently named individuals post, and by how much of a network they already have — there's no fixed timeline we'd responsibly promise. What we can say is that the underlying mechanism (personal profiles reaching further than company pages) is structural and immediate, not something that needs months to "kick in" — the more common delay is operational, in actually getting a consistent capture-and-post rhythm established.

**Isn't this just asking staff to share more content?**
No, and this is a distinction worth being precise about. Employee advocacy — staff sharing and engaging with existing company content — is a genuinely useful secondary lever, but it amplifies content that already exists. The core argument in this article is about where content originates in the first place: whether your firm's expertise is being published from a named individual's own profile, or written and posted as a company page and then, at best, shared by staff afterward. The second is a much weaker starting position than the first.

## The Key Takeaway

A rough, unscripted post outperforming months of company-page content isn't a fluke — it's LinkedIn's own distribution mechanics telling you, consistently, where your firm's real credibility actually sits. For a professional services firm specifically, where clients hire a person's judgement rather than a logo, that gap is worth closing properly rather than working around.

The company page and personal profiles aren't in competition. Once the second is actually being used, the first has a real job to do supporting it.
