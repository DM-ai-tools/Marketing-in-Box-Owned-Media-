### 1B. Cross-Competitor Pattern Analysis

**Table stakes (every credible show in this space has some version — include, don't lead with):**
- A named, credible host or founder, not an anonymous brand account.
- A consistent publishing cadence and a real back-catalogue, not a one-off.
- Some tactical, actionable content in most episodes — audiences expect to leave with at least one thing to try.

**Owned by only one or two competitors (opportunity, not yet saturated):**
- Vertical specificity to accounting/professional services (Marketing for Accounting Firms, With Interest) — but neither combines it with social-media-specific diagnostic depth.
- Podcast-as-relationship-building rather than podcast-as-reach-building (the McIntyre episode on Marketing for Accounting Firms) — a genuinely good idea, currently a single guest segment rather than a whole show's operating philosophy.

**Owned by zero competitors (the biggest gap — highest leverage):**
- **The teardown format.** Not one of the four identified shows walks through a real or composite firm's actual social presence live, in audio, and names the specific mechanism behind what's working or not. Every show is either tips-based (SociaBull), interview-based (Marketing for Accounting Firms, The Numbers Game), or institutional-topic-based (With Interest). Dani has explicitly told us this exact format — the audio/video teardown — is the one thing that gets her full attention, and nobody in the competitive set is doing it.
- **AU professional-services diagnostic framing, specifically for social media.** Marketing for Accounting Firms has the vertical; nobody has the vertical *and* the "which of several distinct causes is actually broken" framing *and* an Australian, partner-led, billable-hour-constrained lens.
- **Partner-time-as-the-real-constraint, named explicitly on-air.** Every competitor implicitly assumes unlimited producer time. None name billable-hour scarcity as the central design constraint of the advice they're giving — which is precisely the objection that kills every proposal Dani has ever been pitched.

**Questions the ICP has that no competitor episode answers:**
- "Is this a strategy problem, a content problem, a capacity problem, or a measurement problem — and how do I even tell the difference?" (Answered nowhere in the competitive set; answered directly by Traffic Radius's own Four Causes framework, not yet brought to audio.)
- "Why does one rough, unscripted partner post outperform six months of our actual content calendar?" (Touched nowhere — this is the single most specific, ownable question in the entire competitive landscape.)
- "How do other firms actually engineer partner participation down to something that survives lodgement season?" (Nobody addresses partner-time cost as a design constraint at all.)

**Structural patterns that work vs. drag:**
- Works: a strong, specific cold open built on a real, relatable moment (closest analogue: Marketing for Accounting Firms' best guest segments, when a guest opens with a sharp specific anecdote rather than a bio).
- Drags: generic platform-feature-of-the-week content (SociaBull) that doesn't build across episodes — each one is disposable rather than compounding into a body of authority.
- Drags: institutional safety (With Interest) — technically correct, forgettable, indistinguishable from every other association touchpoint the ICP already receives.
- Works, underused: guest-as-practitioner-peer tone (The Numbers Game) — worth taking as a texture, even inside a solo-hosted show, by quoting and dramatising real (anonymised) client moments rather than staying abstract.

**Why the opening must be different:** every competitor either opens with a warm generalist welcome (SociaBull), a guest bio (Marketing for Accounting Firms), or an institutional framing statement (With Interest). None open mid-scene, inside a specific, already-happened moment. Traffic Radius's episode must open exactly there — inside the airport-post moment — before the listener knows what show they're even listening to.

### 1C. Synthesis Strategy

- **Table stakes covered, not led with:** a named, credible host (Raj Sri); a real publishing cadence (fortnightly, see Part 2); at least one concrete, usable takeaway per episode.
- **Gap owned exclusively:** the audio teardown format, applied specifically to partner-led professional services firms, using a named diagnostic framework, with partner-time-cost engineered into every recommendation on-air. No competitor identified in Part 1A does any one of these three things; nobody does all three.
- **Single most important insight the ICP takes away:** the reason a rough, 30-second phone post beats six months of a content calendar isn't luck or algorithm — it's a specific, nameable mechanism (where credibility actually sits vs. where the firm is publishing), and once you can see the mechanism, you can point resources at it instead of producing more content that gets the same shrug.
- **The conversation hook:** this show does, live, in audio, what every other show in the category only describes — it opens the hood on one real (or composite, always disclosed) firm's actual social presence and names the specific, evidenced cause behind what's happening, the way a diagnostic consultation would, for free, every episode.
- **Client-branded framework owned from this episode:** **The Signal Ledger** — a simple two-column diagnostic (Where the Attention Actually Is vs. Where the Firm Is Actually Publishing) used as the show's signature teardown tool. It deliberately echoes the language of Dani's own world — ledgers, columns, reconciliation — and sits alongside, not in competition with, the existing Four Causes framework: the Signal Ledger is the *tool* used on-air to work out which of the Four Causes is actually in play for the firm being torn down that episode.

# PART 2 — SHOW CONCEPT & SERIES STRATEGY

### 2A. Show Concept

**Show Name: Billable Attention**

Specific, memorable, and impossible to confuse with a generic marketing podcast. It names the exact tension living inside Dani's world — partner time costed in billable hours, spent (or not spent) on the one activity, attention, that her whole function is accountable for. Searchable, ownable, and instantly signals "this is for professional services, not retail."

**Show Tagline:** *The professional services growth show that opens the hood on real marketing — and tells you which cause is actually yours.*

**Show Premise (2–3 sentences):** Every fortnight, Traffic Radius pulls apart one real or composite professional services firm's actual social media presence, live, in audio — using the same Signal Ledger diagnostic used in paid client engagements — and names the specific, evidenced cause behind what's working and what isn't. It exists because every other show in this category either teaches generic tactics or talks in safe institutional generalities; nobody currently opens the hood, live, on a real firm's numbers and names the mechanism.

**Target Listener:** Dani Whitlock and marketing leads like her — the senior-enough, sophisticated-enough marketing owner inside a 20–150 person Melbourne (or AU) professional services firm who has already tried the obvious things on social, can prove it isn't working, and cannot yet name why. She subscribes because the show speaks to her at her actual maturity level rather than re-explaining what a content calendar is. She keeps coming back because each episode is a fresh, specific case rather than a repeat of last episode's advice — and because the show has, more than once, said something on-air that named a pattern she hadn't put into words herself yet.

**Host Positioning:** Trusted diagnostic advisor, not a hype-building guru and not a generic "content coach." Raj Sri's positioning on the show mirrors Traffic Radius's own established market position (per the CRO Audit and Webinar Package): own the diagnosis, not the delivery. The host is the person in the room who's seen this exact pattern enough times to name it fast — and who will say, on-air, when social isn't the actual problem.

**Episode Cadence Recommendation:** Fortnightly. Weekly is unsustainable against a teardown format that requires real sourcing and case preparation per episode (a genuine differentiator degrades fast if rushed); monthly loses the compounding-authority effect and the show falls out of a commute-listening habit. Fortnightly matches the production capacity implied by Traffic Radius's team (per the Content Marketing Strategy's role list) and matches how Dani actually consumes this category — regularly, but not daily.

**Segment 2: The Signal Ledger**
Duration: 7 minutes
CRO Layer: Serotonin + Dopamine

Script:
"Here's the tool we use with clients to answer exactly that question, and you can run it yourself, right now, on your own firm. We call it the Signal Ledger, and it's deliberately built like a ledger, because you already think in these terms. Two columns. Column one: where is the attention actually coming from — the likes, the comments, the DMs, the 'saw your post' comments in real meetings. Column two: where is the firm actually publishing its effort — which accounts, which voices, which format. In a healthy firm, those two columns roughly reconcile. The attention shows up where the effort is going. In this firm, they don't reconcile at all. Column one — where the real attention is — is almost entirely concentrated on individual partners' personal posts, when they happen, however infrequently. Column two — where the actual effort goes — is almost entirely the firm's company page. Ninety percent of the production hours, ten percent of the real signal. That gap, between the two columns, is the whole story. It's not that the company page content is bad. It's that in this specific category — professional services, where a client is choosing to trust a person with something serious, a business sale, a tax position, a legal exposure — credibility was never going to live on a logo in the first place. It lives with the specific human being who'll actually do the work. The Signal Ledger doesn't tell you to make better company content. It tells you that you've been running the ledger against the wrong account the whole time. And once you see that gap in black and white — because we do write this down, literally, in two columns, in the actual consultation — the eleven-likes result stops being confusing. It becomes completely predictable."

Engagement note for editing: consider a brief pause or subtle audio cue after "ten percent of the real signal" to let the number land before continuing.

Transition Line: "Once you can see that, the next question is obvious — is this just true for this one firm, or is it structural?"

**Segment 3: Naming the Cause**
Duration: 6 minutes
CRO Layer: Serotonin (relief/validation)

Script:
"It's structural, and here's why it matters that it's structural rather than a one-off fluke. We've talked before, on this show and elsewhere, about four distinct causes behind flat social performance — channel-fit, brand-architecture, operating-model, and measurement. What you just heard is brand-architecture, and it is the single most common cause we see in partner-led professional services firms specifically, more than the other three combined.
