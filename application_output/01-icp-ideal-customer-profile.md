## 1. Identity Snapshot

Rachel Nguyen is the Head of Client Services at a full-service digital marketing agency based in Richmond, Melbourne, employing around 95 staff across strategy, SEO, paid media, web development, and account management. The agency has been trading for close to a decade and has built a solid book of mid-market retail, hospitality, and professional services clients across Victoria and interstate. Rachel sits one level below the two co-founders/directors and is the primary internal owner of delivery quality across all client accounts — including social media, which the agency has traditionally run as a "value-add" service bundled into broader retainers rather than a specialised, resourced discipline.

The agency's current social media delivery is handled by a small internal team of two junior-to-mid content coordinators, supplemented by account managers who "help out" when things get busy. There is no dedicated social strategist, and the team relies on a patchwork of scheduling tools, shared drives, and ad hoc briefs rather than a repeatable production system. As an Intermediate-maturity organisation, the agency has established processes for its core services (SEO, paid media) but social media has never had the same investment — it's treated as a cost centre that occasionally causes friction rather than a growth lever.

Procurement at this size is moderately complex: any external spend over a few thousand dollars a month typically needs sign-off from one of the two directors, and Rachel would need to build a business case (cost vs. risk vs. client retention) before bringing in an outside provider. She is not the final decision-maker but is the person who will identify, shortlist, and champion a solution internally.

## 2. Current Reality (What's Happening Right Now)

Rachel is fielding a growing number of uncomfortable conversations. A retail client flagged that their Instagram engagement has flatlined for two months. A hospitality client asked why their competitor's TikTok content looks "so much more current" than what the agency is producing. Internally, one of her two social coordinators just handed in notice, and the remaining coordinator is now covering eleven client accounts solo, working late most Thursdays and Fridays to get content calendars out the door.

Rachel has started keeping a private list of "at-risk" accounts where social media quality — not SEO, not paid media — is the stated or implied reason for client dissatisfaction. She hasn't yet framed this as "we need to outsource social" or "we need a specialist SMM partner" — right now it's simply logged in her head as "social is becoming a liability for the agency" and "we keep losing good social people and it disrupts everything." She's aware something structural needs to change but hasn't landed on what that something is.

## 4. What's Actually True Under the Hood

Underneath the day-to-day fire-fighting, Rachel is carrying real commercial exposure that she hasn't fully articulated upward to the directors yet. Social media, despite being treated as a low-priority add-on, is now touching client retention — and churn risk on even one mid-tier retainer client could represent a meaningful dent in monthly recurring revenue. The agency's utilisation model means every hour her account managers spend "helping out" on social is an hour not billed against strategy or reporting work they're supposed to be doing, quietly eroding margin on those accounts.

There's also a reputational risk she's acutely aware of but hasn't voiced directly: as a digital marketing agency, being visibly weak at social media undermines the agency's own credibility in new business pitches. Prospective clients sometimes ask to see the agency's own social presence or case studies, and right now that's not a strong asset. Staff turnover in the social team is a symptom of a deeper issue — junior coordinators are being asked to do strategist-level thinking without training, support, or realistic workloads, and burnout is the predictable result. Rachel knows that simply hiring a replacement will likely repeat the same cycle within 12–18 months unless the underlying delivery model changes.

## 5. Goals and Desired Outcomes

**Functional Goals**
- Stabilise social media delivery so content goes out consistently, on-brief, and on schedule across all client accounts.
- Reduce the burden on account managers who are currently absorbing overflow social work outside their job scope.
- Build a repeatable process or resourcing model for social that doesn't collapse every time one staff member leaves.

**Emotional Goals**
- Stop dreading client check-in calls where social performance might come up.
- Feel confident presenting the agency's social capability in new business pitches rather than downplaying it.
- Regain a sense of control over delivery quality instead of constantly reacting to complaints.

**Lifestyle/Operational Goals**
- Free up her own time from firefighting social issues so she can focus on higher-value client strategy work.
- Protect the wellbeing and retention of her existing team by removing unsustainable workload pressure.
- Give the directors confidence that client retention risk tied to social is being actively managed, not ignored.

## 6. Pains, Fears, and Risks

Rachel's most pressing fear is losing a client relationship over something that, in her view, is a fixable operational gap rather than a fundamental service failure. There's real revenue risk here — client churn on even one or two mid-sized retainers would be noticed by the directors and could reflect on her performance as Head of Client Services. There's also implementation risk in whatever she proposes: if she pushes for a new hire and it doesn't solve the underlying inconsistency, she'll have spent budget without fixing the problem, and her judgement will be questioned.

She's wary of vendor lock-in or dependency on an external party she doesn't fully trust yet — having an outside provider "own" a visible, client-facing function like social media feels higher-stakes than something more back-of-house like technical SEO audits. There's also a quieter fear about capability gaps being exposed: bringing in outside help could imply to the directors (or to clients, if handled clumsily) that the agency isn't as full-service as it presents itself to be. She hasn't ruled out that fear, even though logically she knows plenty of agencies use specialist partners behind the scenes.

## 7. Problems They Face Getting What They Need

Because Rachel hasn't yet framed her problem as "we need a social media partner," her search behaviour (if she searches at all) is scattered — she might look up "how to reduce agency staff turnover," "social media workflow tools for agencies," or "why is client social engagement dropping," rather than searching for a provider directly. This makes it genuinely hard for her to discover that outsourced or white-label social media support for agencies is even a viable category, since most content she encounters is aimed at brands managing their own social, not agencies needing overflow or specialist support.

Trust and credibility are also difficult to assess from the outside: agency-to-agency service arrangements are rarely advertised publicly, so she has limited visibility into who does this well, what quality looks like, or how confidentiality and client-facing representation would be handled (would the client know? would work be branded as the agency's own?). Comparing options is hard because pricing for this kind of arrangement is rarely published, and she has no easy way to benchmark whether a proposed cost represents good value relative to hiring internally. There's also a logistical concern about handover — how would briefs, brand guidelines, and approvals actually flow between her team and an external partner without adding more coordination overhead, which is precisely the burden she's trying to reduce.

## 8. Barriers and Uncertainties (Why They're Stuck)

Rachel is stuck partly because the problem still feels solvable through internal means — "just hire another coordinator" is the default assumption inside the business, even though she suspects it won't hold. She hasn't yet built a confident business case to bring to the directors, and raising the idea of bringing in an external party for a client-facing function feels like it needs stronger justification than "our current team is stretched." There's also a misbelief at play: that outsourcing social media necessarily means lower quality or less brand control, which makes it feel riskier than it may actually be. Without a clear framework for evaluating alternatives, she defaults to inaction and incremental fixes — covering shifts, redistributing work, delaying the conversation with the directors — rather than addressing the structural issue.

There is a clear, underserved niche here: most social media marketing messaging in the Melbourne market is aimed directly at brands, not at agencies needing overflow capacity, specialist support, or white-label delivery for their own client base. TrafficRadius has an opportunity to speak directly to agency operations leaders like Rachel using language that acknowledges agency-specific pressures — staff turnover, utilisation, client retention risk, and pitch credibility — rather than generic "grow your followers" messaging.

A resource such as "Why Agencies Struggle to Keep Social Media Consistent (And What Usually Breaks First)" would meet her exactly where she is, building trust before introducing the idea that specialist, flexible external support is a legitimate and low-risk option for agencies in her position.
