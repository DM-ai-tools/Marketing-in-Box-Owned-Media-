The 4 Formulas
What makes each type work — and when to use it
Specificity Stack
Exact numbers beat vague claims every time
The brain trusts “73 left” far more than “only a few remaining.” Specific numbers signal that someone actually counted — which means it’s probably true. Round numbers feel estimated. Odd numbers feel measured.
Formula: [specific quantity] + [specific remaining or timeframe]
Honest Signal
Admitting a limitation builds more trust than hiding it
When a brand acknowledges something that works against its interests — a rising price, a limit on production, a cheaper alternative — it disarms the “this is a sales pitch” reflex. The reader lowers their guard because the brand clearly isn’t trying to hide anything.
Formula: [uncomfortable truth] + [straightforward reason]
Tension Opener
An unresolved incongruity keeps the brain engaged
The human brain instinctively tries to resolve tension. A headline that creates a mild incongruity — something slightly off, surprising, or counter-intuitive — forces the reader to keep going to resolve it. The key is “mild”: it should create curiosity, not confusion.
Formula: [unexpected juxtaposition] that creates a question the reader has to answer
Social Validator
AU-specific social proof closes the credibility gap faster
“14,283 Australians” is more credible than “14,283 people.” Location and national identity make social proof feel verifiable and local. The specificity compounds the effect — the reader mentally checks whether they might know someone in that number.
Formula: [AU-specific quantity or location] + [social behaviour or outcome]
